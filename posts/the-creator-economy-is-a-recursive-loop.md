I recently noticed something strange about the content I consume.

A creator makes a video about how much money they make from YouTube. That video performs well, so they make another video explaining how they made the first video perform well. Then they launch a course teaching other people how to make similar videos. Some of those people start their own channels documenting what they learned from the course.

Eventually, one of them makes a video titled:

**"How I Made Money by Following a Creator Who Teaches People How to Make Money."**

And the loop continues.

The creator economy is not just an economy. It is a recursive system — one that constantly creates new participants by talking about itself.

## Content creates creators

Traditional media had a fairly clear division.

A small group of people created the content. Everyone else consumed it.

You watched a film, read a newspaper, or listened to the radio. You might have enjoyed the experience, but you probably did not finish a movie and immediately think, "I should start my own production company."

The creator economy works differently.

You watch someone build a business on YouTube, and they tell you that you can do it too. You listen to a podcast about writing online, and the host explains how you can start your own newsletter. You read a LinkedIn post about personal branding, and by the end of it, you are mentally drafting your next post.

The content does not just entertain or inform you. It recruits you.

Every consumer is a potential creator, and every creator becomes proof that the opportunity exists.

That is the first loop:

**Creators make content, people consume it, some consumers become creators, and they make more content.**

Platforms love this because they never have to produce anything themselves. Their users create the supply, consume the supply, and then convince more people to join the supply side.

It is an incredibly efficient machine.

## The product is often the possibility

A large part of the creator economy runs on aspiration.

The apartment tour is not only about the apartment. It is about the life that appears to come with it.

The travel vlog is not only about Bali. It is about the possibility of earning money while travelling.

The "day in my life" video is not only a record of someone's day. It is a demonstration of what your day could look like.

And a video titled "How I Made ₹5 Lakh From Content Creation" is not only sharing information. It is selling the possibility that you could do the same.

The most interesting part is that the video itself helps the creator make more money. Their proof becomes their marketing, and their marketing creates more proof.

A creator earns money by talking about how they earn money.

The more money that content generates, the more credible their next video about making money becomes.

It is almost perfectly recursive.

## Success becomes content, and content creates success

Imagine someone starts a YouTube channel about productivity.

At first, they make videos about note-taking apps, morning routines, and time management. After a while, the channel grows.

Now they have a new topic to talk about: how they grew the channel.

Those videos might perform even better than the original productivity videos because they attract people who want to become creators themselves.

The creator can then sell a course, launch a community, recommend software through affiliate links, or offer coaching.

At this point, they are no longer only a productivity creator. They are also a creator who teaches people how to become productivity creators.

Their success has become content.

That content creates more success.

That success becomes more content.

You can see the same pattern everywhere:

- Freelancers teaching people how to become freelancers
- Newsletter writers writing newsletters about growing newsletters
- Founders building products for people who want to build products
- Personal-brand experts building personal brands by teaching personal branding
- Creators selling courses about how to sell courses

The closer you look, the more the internet starts to resemble a hall of mirrors.

## Everyone is selling a map

None of this automatically means that the advice is useless.

Someone who has genuinely built an audience probably does understand things that a beginner does not. Learning from experienced people can save months of trial and error.

But the incentives are worth understanding.

During a gold rush, selling maps and tools can sometimes be more reliable than searching for gold.

In the creator economy, teaching people how to become creators can be more profitable than creating content about almost anything else.

Why?

Because the audience is not only buying information. They are buying hope, leverage, independence, status, and the possibility of escaping a job they do not enjoy.

That makes "how to become a creator" an unusually powerful category.

## I am part of the loop too

I cannot pretend that I am observing this from the outside.

I watch project-building videos and immediately want to build something.

I see someone improving their GitHub profile and start thinking about my next repository.

I watch creators explain how writing online changed their careers, and then I open a document to write a blog about the creator economy.

This blog itself is part of the loop.

I am writing content about people who make content about making content.

That is what makes the creator economy so fascinating. You cannot study it without participating in it. Even criticism becomes content, and if that criticism performs well, the person criticising the system benefits from the system.

The loop absorbs almost everything.

## The loop is not the problem

I do not think the recursive nature of the creator economy makes it fake.

In many ways, it is what makes the internet exciting.

Someone learns a skill, shares what they learned, and helps another person start. That person experiments, discovers something new, and shares it with the next person.

Knowledge compounds because people document the process.

A developer builds a small project and posts the code. Someone else improves it. A marketer shares a campaign breakdown. Another person tests the idea in a different industry. A filmmaker explains a technique, and thousands of people gain access to knowledge that once belonged only to professionals.

That is recursion at its best.

The problem is not that creators teach other creators.

The problem is when teaching becomes a substitute for doing.

There should be something at the centre of the loop: an actual skill, experiment, product, story, obsession, or body of work.

Otherwise, the recursion becomes empty.

You are no longer creating because you have something to say. You are creating content about how to create content so that you can eventually earn enough money to continue creating content about creating content.

The loop keeps moving, but it is not going anywhere.

## Create something outside the loop

Maybe the best way to participate in the creator economy is to begin with something that would still interest you even if nobody were watching.

Build a project.

Learn a strange skill.

Start a business.

Review films.

Restore cars.

Travel somewhere unfamiliar.

Run an experiment.

Become unusually good at something.

Then create content around the experience.

This gives the content a source. You are not endlessly repeating advice that already exists. You are bringing new evidence into the system.

The most interesting creators are rarely interesting only because they understand content. They are interesting because content gives us access to the way they see something else.

The creator economy will continue feeding itself. More consumers will become creators. More creators will teach other people how to create. More success stories will produce more success stories.

The loop is not going to stop.

But if you want to enter it, bring something with you.

Otherwise, we might all end up teaching one another how to build audiences, with nothing left to say once they arrive.
